Cache consistency

Lesson 1 showed product's cache working: copies of MongoDB answers, kept in Redis with a throw-away time. This lesson is about what happens to those copies when a product changes. You'll watch a real search show an old price in the cluster, force a rarer race on your laptop, and design the fix.

Where this lesson starts

Recall check: Lesson 1 in 2 minutes

From memory, before you look: what were Lesson 1's three "In short" ideas?

Hint: three steps, a label, a copy.

Show the answers
  1. Cache-aside: look, fetch, store. The product pod looks in Redis; on a miss it reads MongoDB and stores a copy with a TTL. Redis never fetches anything.
  2. The key is the question. A product page is product:<id>; a search is the whole query, sorted and fingerprinted, under product_search:v2:.
  3. The cache is a copy, not the truth. MongoDB is the system of record, and the TTL limits how long a copy can be wrong.

Missed one? Reread section 5 of Lesson 1 (photocopies on the whiteboard). This lesson picks up exactly where that picture ended.

What "consistent" means here

A cache is consistent when its copy says the same thing as the original. A Kindle that costs 129 in MongoDB and 129 in Redis: consistent. 129 in MongoDB and 149 in Redis: the copy is out of date, and every shopper who gets the copy sees the wrong price. Nothing crashes. It's just wrong.

Why it matters in our shop

Sellers change prices and stock all the time, through PATCH /api/product/:id. Every change makes some copies in Redis out of date. product does clean up after a change, but only one of its two kinds of copy. Search results are never cleaned up, so after a price change a search can show the old price for up to an hour. That bug is in the code today, and you'll see it in the real cluster.

Why this lesson comes now

Lesson 1 ended on one sentence: if someone edits the original in the filing room, the photocopy on the whiteboard doesn't change. This lesson is that sentence, run for real. Lesson 3 then asks the opposite question: what happens when lots of copies vanish at once?

By the end of this lesson you'll be able to:

  • say exactly which copies product cleans up after a change, and which it misses;
  • explain how a slow reader can put an old copy back after a clean-up, and why the TTL is what finally fixes it;
  • design a way to make every search copy out of date at once, without finding each one.

No seller account? How this lesson was run

The author had no seller account and didn't create one in the shared database. Instead, the cookie was made the way the auth tests make one: a token signed with the cluster's own secret, inside the product pod, with the admin role:

TOKEN=$(kubectl exec deploy/product-deployment -c product -- node -e \
  "process.stdout.write(require('jsonwebtoken').sign({id:'000000000000000000000001',email:'lesson2@example.com',role:'admin'},process.env.JWT_KEY))")
printf 'session=%s' "$(printf '{"jwt":"%s"}' "$TOKEN" | base64 | tr -d '\n')" > seller.txt

Why not the Kindle from Lesson 1?

Changing the Kindle's price returns 500. 1 · The story: a price drop

This is the real product service and a real product. The sale is made up.

  1. Before · works

    The Tanjiro figure costs 299. Shoppers open its page and search for "tanjiro". Both answers are copied into Redis: the page for 15 minutes, the search for 60.

  2. Then · a change

    The seller drops the price to 249 for a weekend sale. product saves it in MongoDB and takes the page's copy out of Redis, so the next page view fetches the new price.

  3. The catch · hidden

    Nobody takes the search copy down. For up to an hour, the search list says 299 and the page says 249. A price drop is merely confusing. A price rise is worse: the list shows a lower price than the one charged at checkout. No error, no log line, nothing on any dashboard.

Before you run it, a guess.

Commit to an answer first.

The change removes the page's copy, so the next page view fetches 249. Nothing touches the search copy. Run section 3 to watch it.

Did anything else notice the change?

Yes. After saving, product also sends a product-updated message over RabbitMQ, so other services can update their copies of the product. In the run above, cart's log said ProductUpdatedListener: product 696099a9… not found — discarding message: no cart held that product, so there was nothing to update. Those copies in other services are the same problem one level up, and section 9 comes back to them.

4 · Slow motion: one price change

Here is step 3 again, with the parts Redis couldn't see filled in:

  1. productMongoDB: findById, set price 249, savesaved
  2. productRedis: DEL product:6960…31651
  3. productRabbitMQ: publish product-updatedsent

Why doesn't product delete the search copy too? Ask yourself which key it would delete. The page's key is easy: product: plus the id it already has. A search key is a fingerprint of a question: "tanjiro", "figure", "category=other", "price under 300", page 2 of something. Any of them might list this product. The code can't work backwards from a product to every question whose answer contains it, and fingerprints can't be turned back into questions anyway.

5 · The second way: a slow reader

The page's copy was deleted, so the page is safe. Or is it? There's a rarer way for even that copy to go stale, and it needs two pods at once. This is where the lab Redis comes in.

A shopper's pod misses the cache and starts loading the price from MongoDB (149). While it's loading, a seller changes the price to 129 and deletes the key. Then the shopper's pod finishes and saves what it loaded. What does the next shopper see?

The delete came first; the old copy was saved after it. Nothing deletes it again, so it stays until its throw-away time. Run the lab to see it.

  1. Run the race. In the lab folder:

    npm run lab:02 -- --broken

Broken: delete the key on write · TTL 5s

  reader loaded:     149 (from store)
  seller set:        129
  store now:         129
  cache key:         lab:02:cache:kindle
  next shopper sees: 149 (from cache) ✗

Shoppers keep reading, once a second
  after 1s:          149 (from cache) ✗
  after 2s:          149 (from cache) ✗
  after 3s:          149 (from cache) ✗
  after 4s:          149 (from cache) ✗
  after 5s:          129 (from store) ✓

    The store says 129, the cache says 149, and every shopper gets 149 until the copy's 5-second TTL runs out. Then a miss loads 129, and everything is right again. In the shop, that TTL would be 15 minutes.

  2. Run it without the forced timing. The lab normally holds the shopper at the worst moment. --no-pause lets the two workers just run:

    npm run lab:02 -- --broken --no-pause --ttl 1
  …
  next shopper sees: 129 (from cache) ✓

    Three runs out of three came out right. The shopper's load finished too late to read the old price. That's why this bug survives testing: it needs a read that is slower than a whole write, at exactly the wrong moment. Under real load (a slow MongoDB, a garbage-collection pause, a busy node) that moment does come.

How does the lab hold the shopper at the worst moment?

With a pause point, a lab helper in lib/pauses.ts. The shopper calls pauseAt('reader-loaded') right after loading from the store. The seller calls waitUntilPaused('reader-loaded'), does its whole update, then resume('reader-loaded'). Lesson 0's starting gun lines everyone up; a pause point holds one worker while another goes in between. Both are just Redis lists and BLPOP, under lab:02:.

6 · Why: the copy in someone's hands

Lesson 1's filing room and photocopies come back, with one thing added.

When an original changes in the filing room, you take its photocopy off the whiteboard. That works for the copy you can find by its label.

But some photocopies are of lists: "every figure under 300", "everything matching tanjiro". The changed item is on some of them, and their labels don't say which. You can't take down what you can't find.

And a colleague may be walking back from the filing room right now, holding a photocopy made before the change. You clear the board; they pin their old copy up a minute later. The board is wrong again, and nobody knows.

The fix is a number at the top of the board: "current edition: 1". Every label carries the edition it was made for. When an original changes, you don't hunt for copies; you change the number to 2. Old copies can stay pinned up. Nobody reads them, because nobody asks for edition 1 any more, and they come down at their throw-away time.

taking a copy down
DEL product:<id>, what updateProduct does.
photocopies of lists
the product_search:v2:* keys. A key is a fingerprint of a question, so it can't tell you which products are in its answer.
the colleague walking back
a pod that read MongoDB before the change and saves its copy after the delete.
the throw-away time
the TTL. It's what finally cleared the board in both cases, and why every copy must have one.
the edition number
a counter in Redis. Readers put it into every key; writers INCR it after a change.

7 · The fix: change the edition, not the copies

The fixed lab is the same race with one change. The shopper reads the edition number first and puts it in the key (…:kindle:g0). The seller INCRs the number after saving, instead of deleting.

  1. Run the same race, fixed.

    npm run lab:02 -- --fixed

Fixed: bump the edition number on write · TTL 5s
  store before:      149
  reader loaded:     149 (from store)
  seller set:        129
  store now:         129
  cache key:         lab:02:cache:kindle:g1
  next shopper sees: 129 (from store) ✓
  old copy:          lab:02:cache:kindle:g0 = 149, TTL 5s, never read again

    The shopper still saved the old price, under edition 0. By then the edition was 1, so the next shopper asked for …:g1, missed, and loaded 129. The old copy sits there unread until its TTL takes it down.

  2. Check both claims.

    npm run lab:02 -- --check
  …
Check
  broken: next shopper gets the old price: yes ✓
  broken: stale for the whole TTL, then fresh: yes ✓
  fixed: the old price was cached under edition 0: yes ✓
  fixed: next shopper gets the new price: yes ✓
  no keys left under lab:02:*: yes ✓

Two details make it safe, and both matter. The shopper reads the edition before loading from the store, so an old load is always filed under an old edition. The seller bumps the edition after the store has saved, so nobody can load the old price under the new edition. Swap either order and the race comes back.

It isn't free: every read now costs one extra round trip (read the number, then the copy), and every change makes all copies under that number useless, including ones that were still right.

Designing the fix for product

This course designs the fix; applying it to product is a separate follow-up. Here's the design, key by key:

product_search:gen · new
one edition number for all search copies. A search key can't say which products it lists, so any change to any product must make every search copy out of date. One INCR does it, however many search copies exist.
product_search:v2:g<gen>:<md5> · was product_search:v2:<md5>
the search key with the edition in it. showProduct reads product_search:gen first, then builds the key. The old copies expire on their own within the hour.
who bumps it
every write that can change a list: updateProduct, deleteProduct, the quantity listener (stock is shown in results, and quantity[gte] filters on it), and newProduct, which today doesn't touch the cache at all, so a new product is missing from cached searches for up to an hour. Always after the MongoDB write has succeeded.
product:<id> · keep the delete
the page's key can be found by its id, so deleting it after the save stays. Only the slow-reader race can beat it, and its 15-minute TTL is the backstop. If even that window is too long, give each product its own edition (product:gen:<id>), exactly as the lab does.
What else was considered, and why not
  • Delete every search key with SCAN MATCH product_search:*, then UNLINK. It walks the whole keyspace on every price change, is slow with many keys, and still loses to the slow-reader race. Never KEYS *: it blocks Redis until it has checked every key.
  • Remember which searches list which product, in a Redis set per product, and delete exactly those. Precise, but every search writes to many sets, and the sets themselves can go stale. Too much machinery for this shop.
  • Just shorten the search TTL. Fewer minutes of wrong prices, more trips to MongoDB, and still wrong for a while. A fine stopgap, not a fix.

The trade-off of the chosen design: a busy shop with constant stock changes would bump the number all the time, and search copies would rarely live long enough to be used. Then you'd move stock out of the cached list, or edition by category. That's a decision for when the numbers say so; the observability work can chart the search hit ratio.

8 · Now name it

You've seen each of these happen. Their proper names, used from here on:

cache invalidation
making a cached copy stop being used after the original changes: deleting it, or making its key unreachable. What updateProduct does for the page, and nothing does for search.
invalidate on write
doing the invalidation as part of every write to the system of record, instead of waiting for the TTL.
delete vs update on write
on a change, either delete the copy (the next read fetches the new one) or write the new value into the cache straight away. Delete is the usual choice; see below.
delete after commit
invalidate only once the database write has succeeded. Delete before it, and a reader can load and re-cache the old value in the gap.
stale set
the race in section 5: a reader that loaded before a change saves its old copy after the invalidation.
backstop TTL
the TTL's real job once you invalidate on write: the longest a copy can stay wrong when an invalidation is missed or beaten by a stale set.
generation counter
the edition number: a counter that is part of every key in a family. INCR it and the whole family is invalidated at once. A versioned key (v2) is the same idea, changed by hand in code instead of by every write.

All of them are in the glossary, and the decision list is in the caching sheet's invalidation section.

Why delete, and not write the new price into the cache?

Two sellers change the same price a moment apart: A to 249, then B to 199. MongoDB ends at 199. If each also SETs its price in Redis, the two SETs can arrive in the other order, B then A, and the cache says 249 until its TTL. That's Lesson 0's lost update, in a cache. Two deletes can't be in the wrong order: either way the key is gone and the next read loads 199. Updating the cache on write (write-through) only works when writes to one key can't overlap.

tells RabbitMQ the message is done, so it isn't sent again. If the pod crashed after the update but before the ack, RabbitMQ would send the message again and the stock would be taken twice. Lesson 5 comes back to this.

A surprise: nothing in the repo sends this message today. product, cart and order all listen for product-quantity-updated, but no service publishes it (search the repo for RoutingKeyTypes.ProductQuantityUpdated: only listeners). product doesn't listen for order-created either, so as far as the code shows, a sale doesn't lower stock in MongoDB at all. When someone adds the publisher, this listener's search gap comes with it.

Other services' copies

cart and order keep their own copy of each product (title, price, stock) in their own databases, updated by product-updated messages. Those copies are caches too, just slower ones: a message delivered late, or lost, leaves them stale, with no TTL to rescue them. Same idea, different tools. The rule from this lesson still holds: decide what clears a stale copy, and what the backstop is.

In short: remember these 3

  1. A change must clear every copy it affects. product deletes product:<id> after each change but never touches product_search:v2:*, so search can show an old price for up to an hour.
  2. A slow reader can put an old copy back. It loads before the change and saves after the delete. Only the TTL clears it, so every copy needs one: the backstop.
  3. When you can't find the copies, change the edition. Put a generation number in every key of a family and INCR it after each change. Old copies are never read again and expire on their own.

If you forget everything: photocopies don't change when the original does; take them down, or change the edition number.

You don't need to memorise the key names, the TTLs or the lab output. The decision list is on the caching sheet, terms in the glossary.

10 · Check yourself

Answer from memory first, then click. You can retry a wrong answer.

Why doesn't updateProduct simply delete the search keys that list the changed product?

The page's key is built from the id the code already has. A search key is an MD5 of the query, and any query might list the product. There's no way back from a product to its search keys.

In the broken lab, the seller's DEL returned 0. What does that tell you?

Nothing was cached at that moment. The shopper had loaded 149 and saved it after the delete. That's the stale set.

In the fixed lab, a teammate moves the INCR of the edition number to before the store save. Is it still safe?

Between the INCR and the save, a reader picks up edition 1 and loads 149 from the store, which hasn't changed yet. Its copy goes under …:g1, the key everyone now reads. Invalidate after commit.

From Lesson 1

The search copy had 3,597 seconds left when the price changed. What decided that number?

A ?search= list is stored with EX 3600; three seconds had passed. The wrapper's 15-minute default is for the product page. Redis never hears from MongoDB.

From Lesson 0

Two sellers change one price a moment apart, and each also SETs its new price in the cache. What can go wrong?

Each SET is atomic on its own, but the order of two writers isn't guaranteed. The later database write can be overwritten in the cache by the earlier one. Deleting instead of updating avoids it.
